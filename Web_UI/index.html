01 · Meet the Species

A Mammal Built for Cold, Cornered by Heat

High in the talus fields of the western mountains lives an animal that never sleeps through winter and cannot survive a hot afternoon.

The American pika (Ochotona princeps) is a small, round-bodied relative of rabbits and hares, though it belongs to its own family, Ochotonidae. Adults measure roughly 16–22 cm long and weigh between 120 and 180 g — small enough to disappear into a gap between two boulders, which is exactly where it spends most of its life.

Pikas are unusual among small mammals in two ways: they are active by day, all year round, and they never hibernate. Instead of sleeping through winter, a pika survives on a haypile — a cache of grasses and wildflowers it spends the entire summer clipping, sun-drying, and stacking under the rocks. Each pika defends its own territory with a sharp, whistling alarm call, often the first sign of a colony long before any animal is seen.

That same lifestyle makes the pika one of the most closely watched indicator species in North American climate science. It cannot dig a burrow to escape the heat, and it cannot easily walk to a cooler mountain range. What it can do is retreat deeper into the talus — and when even that isn't enough, disappear from a site entirely.

02 · The Threat

How a Warming Climate Reaches the Talus

The pika's biology leaves almost no margin for a warmer world. Four pressures, acting together, explain why researchers now track this species so closely.

Extreme Heat Sensitivity

A pika's resting body temperature already sits close to its lethal limit — around 40°C against an upper limit near 43°C. Field studies have found that sustained exposure to air temperatures above roughly 25°C (77°F) for more than about six hours can be fatal. On hot afternoons, pikas retreat into cool crevices deep in the talus, which shortens the time left for feeding and haypile building.

Shrinking Habitat, Fewer Places to Go

As average temperatures rise, the elevation band where pikas can survive shifts upward. A 2021 study of the Northern Rockies found upslope range retraction in 44 of 64 surveyed watersheds, with an average shift of 281 m. Because pikas disperse poorly, populations near the tops of their ranges often have nowhere higher left to retreat to.

Vanishing Snowpack

Winter snow isn't a threat to pikas — it's their blanket. A deep, early snowpack insulates the talus from lethal cold, letting pikas stay active all winter. As snow cover shrinks and arrives later in the season, some populations face a paradox: a warmer world that leaves them more exposed to deadly cold.

Drought and Disappearing Forage

Drier conditions reduce the alpine grasses and wildflowers pikas depend on for both summer grazing and winter haypiles. Researchers have linked low air moisture to reduced plant growth and thinner snowpack at once — a combined effect some recent studies suggest may threaten certain populations even more than heat alone.

The species overall is still classified as Least Concern, but that number hides a sharper local reality: since the early 2000s, researchers have documented pikas disappearing from numerous historically occupied sites across the Great Basin — an early warning for a species that has nowhere left to climb.

03 · The Tool

Turning Climate Data Into a Conservation Signal

You can't monitor every mountain range in person. A species distribution model turns the question “where might pikas still be able to survive?” into something you can calculate directly from climate data.

This tool is built around a classification model. Given the climate, terrain, and drought conditions at a specific place and year, it predicts one of two outcomes: Present or Absent. Trained on real occurrence records — commonly drawn from sources like GBIF — alongside climate layers such as WorldClim and terrain data, algorithms like Random Forest, gradient-boosted trees, or MaxEnt learn the combination of conditions under which pikas are reliably found.

Why a Prediction Matters for Conservation

  • Finds climate refugia — cooler, more stable sites worth protecting first
  • Directs limited field-survey time toward the highest-priority locations
  • Flags at-risk populations before a local extinction is confirmed on the ground
  • Supports habitat corridor and connectivity planning between mountain ranges
  • Gives land managers a quantitative basis for permitting and policy decisions
